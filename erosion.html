Weathering, Mass Wasting, and Erosion

Chile stretches about 4,300 km from the driest desert on Earth to rain-soaked Patagonian fjords, so the same rock can break down in very different ways depending on latitude. This page follows the chain of processes: rock is weathered in place, gravity moves it downslope, and water, wind, and ice carry it away. Jump to: Weathering, Mass wasting, Water erosion, Wind erosion, Deserts, Glaciers.

Weathering

Weathering is controlled by water, temperature, and vegetation, and Chile’s Coastal Cordillera shows this clearly. Researchers measured granite weathering at four sites between 26°S and 38°S, from arid to temperate humid climates (Schaller & Ehlers, 2022). Chemical weathering was essentially zero in the arid north, peaked in the Mediterranean zone, and was moderate in the semi-arid and humid zones (Schaller & Ehlers, 2022).

Hydrolysis and carbonation

Hydrolysis is the main chemical attack on granite. Water reacts with feldspar and turns it into clay. That fits the pattern above: where there is little water in the arid north, chemical weathering is essentially absent, and it increases as rainfall and vegetation increase toward the south (Schaller & Ehlers, 2022). Carbonation happens when carbon dioxide dissolved in rain forms a weak acid that dissolves limestone. The best Chilean example is Madre de Dios Island in Magallanes, whose pure carbonate rock has been shaped into marble karst by 8,000 to 10,000 mm of rain a year (Meteored, n.d.-b). Measurements there suggest the surface dissolves about 3 mm every 50 years (L’équipe Ultima Esperanza & Maire, 1999).

Mass wasting

Steep Andean slopes, heavy storms, earthquakes, and retreating glaciers make landslides and debris flows common. In the arid north a rare storm can be just as destructive: on 24–26 March 2015 intense rain hit the Atacama Desert, and the Salado River flowed at roughly 1,000 m³/s, leaving about 4.5 m of sludge in the city of Chañaral (Falcón Hernández et al., 2017).

Case study: Villa Santa Lucía, 2017

  • 16 December 2017 A 7 million m³ rockslide fell from the Yelcho range beside a retreating glacier. The material became a mudflow that traveled about 8 to 10 km down the Burritos River, destroyed about half of Villa Santa Lucía, and killed 22 people (Somos-Valenzuela et al., 2020).
  • Why it was so destructive The flow picked up water from saturated volcanic soils along its path, so even without a glacial lake it could fluidize and reach the town at about 20 m/s (Somos-Valenzuela et al., 2020).
  • Warning signs The national geology service, SERNAGEOMIN, had mapped moderate to high mass-wasting hazards around the village in 2008, but the town itself was not considered at risk (Somos-Valenzuela et al., 2020).

What the government has done

After the disaster, authorities planned a safer site and rules for the village. The housing ministry’s reconstruction reports describe a proposed area for the new settlement, hydraulic works designed by the public works ministry, and a new land-use plan for Chaitén that includes Villa Santa Lucía (Ministerio de Vivienda y Urbanismo, 2023). Scientists who studied the event urged that future hazard maps combine soil tests with flow simulations (Somos-Valenzuela et al., 2020). At the national level, Chile has hazard-mapping rules (Law 21.364 and Decree 86), but a University of Chile study found a gap between hazard maps made by about twelve technical agencies and local land-use plans (Universidad de Chile, n.d.-b).

Channel types

The best-documented example is the Biobío. A study of its middle reach found it was originally braided, with several channels around bars and islands, but dams built at Pangue (1996), Ralco (2004), and Angostura (2013) trapped sediment and cut floods. The channel narrowed by about 58 to 98 m and became straighter and less sinuous (Salazar Cárdenas, 2019). Farther downstream, an 80 km reach of very gentle slope favors meanders and sediment deposition (Salazar Cárdenas, 2019). I did not find a comparable published channel classification for the Baker or the Loa.

Impacts on communities

  • Floods and growth. The last 25 km of the Biobío, through Hualqui, Chiguayante, Concepción, Hualpén, and San Pedro de la Paz, face a high flood risk (van Heemst et al., 2013). In 2006 a 100-year flood of about 15,700 m³/s put urban areas under water (van Heemst et al., 2013), and about 1.5 million people live in the metropolitan area beside the river (Schoener et al., 2022).
  • Dams and displacement. Building the Pangue dam displaced Pehuenche communities who lived along the river (WorldAtlas, n.d.).
  • Glacial-lake floods on the Baker. In 2008 outburst floods from Lago Cachet 2 pushed the Baker to roughly 3,000–3,600 m³/s, about triple its normal flow (Dussaillant et al., 2010).
  • Santiago’s water. Gravel mining in the Maipo has degraded its banks and threatens the capital’s drinking water, and a 2015 government study already recommended stopping it (La Tercera, n.d.).

Government response

The Dirección de Obras Hidráulicas (DOH) builds riverbank defenses, channels rivers, and clears sediment so channels stay open during floods (Dirección de Obras Hidráulicas, n.d.). Its 2023 winter plan included riprap and channel work on the Maipo and Mapocho (Infraestructura Pública, 2023). In 2025 it invested about 109 billion pesos in bank regularization, river works, and alluvial control, starting works on the Andalién River in Concepción for more than 200,000 beneficiaries (Dirección de Obras Hidráulicas, 2026).

Wind erosion

Yes. On the Mejillones Peninsula in the coastal Atacama, strong winds along the coast erode the flat surface and carry sand out to sea (Flores-Aqueveque et al., 2010). To reduce soil loss, the farm agency INDAP funds conservation practices, including tree windbreaks, in communes with highly eroded soils. In 2025 it reached 44 of 131 such communes (Instituto de Desarrollo Agropecuario, 2026).

Deserts and desertification

The Atacama Desert covers much of northern Chile. Its hyperarid core gets less than 2 mm of rain a year (Ritter, 2019). It lies between the Coastal Range on the west and the Andean foothills on the east (Encyclopaedia Britannica, n.d.-c).

Different agencies measure this differently. CONAF’s 2016 assessment counted 76 communes as severely and 108 as moderately affected, and flagged Aysén and Magallanes among the worst regions (Corporación Nacional Forestal [CONAF] & Sud Austral Consulting, 2016). Experts also stress that the expansion of monoculture farming drives it, not only climate (CIPER, 2022). The response includes CONAF’s national strategy on climate change and vegetation and a national action program against desertification (UNCCD, 2026).

Glaciers

Yes, both ancient and modern. During the last glacial cycle the Patagonian Ice Sheet reached from 38°S to 56°S and covered about 492,600 km² at 35,000 years ago (Rodriguez et al., 2023). It carved cirques, U-shaped valleys, and the deep fjords of the southern coast, and left moraines behind (Rodriguez et al., 2023).

Present-day ice

The Southern Patagonian Ice Field covers about 13,000 km² and feeds outlet glaciers that end in fjords and lakes (U.S. Geological Survey, n.d.-c).

How glacial features affect Chile

  • Hazards. Retreating glaciers expose unstable slopes and create lakes, which can burst. The Yelcho glacier had retreated at least 250 m in the decade before the Villa Santa Lucía disaster (Somos-Valenzuela et al., 2020), and the Baker floods in 2008 came from a glacial lake (Dussaillant et al., 2010).
  • Water and rivers. Meltwater feeds big rivers like the Baker, whose basin is largely covered by the Northern Patagonian Icefield on its Andean slope (Dussaillant et al., 2012). General Carrera Lake, a glacial lake, is the second largest in South America (Dussaillant et al., 2012).
  • Volcanic ice. Villarrica carries about 40 km² of glaciers, so eruptions can trigger lahars (Volcano World, n.d.).
